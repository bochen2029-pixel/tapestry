## §1 · The dataset question, settled first

The operator recalled "something better than Olist, something about Chicago". **There is no Chicago dataset in the record.** Searched with `everywhen` over 1,881 indexed session tapes: the phrase `chicago` at 5 days and at all time (every hit is the timezone in a session-limit message), then `311`, `service requests`, `taxi trips`, `open data portal`, `socrata`, `building permits`, `food inspections` over 10 days — zero hits each — then a semantic `seek` across 28,669 embedded messages for a better-than-Olist dataset, which returned only OrgSolver threads **[M]**.

What the search **did** find is the real verdict on Olist, in the `C--ABC` session of 2026-09-08 **[R]**: Olist reads *about zero decision headroom* — "physics, no decision" — and Home Credit was tried next (307,511 loan applications, +0.066 AUC over self-report, of which only +0.005 beyond the credit score a human already sees). The strategic finding there was that public datasets are systematically **already instrumented**, so they return the floor.

**That verdict does not transfer to this gate, and the distinction matters.** Falsifier 19 is not a headroom measurement. It asks whether the store reproduces the source tables — whether the ledger agrees with the world — and for that the requirement is a messy real wire with joins, revisions and multi-row groups, not a domain with exploitable signal. Olist is not merely adequate here; it is **the dataset falsifier 19 was written against**: an order has many line items, so an order's amount exists *only* if somebody declared `sum`, and the falsifier's planted lie is "last-row-wins on a summed column".

If the operator can name the Chicago set, swapping it in is a `TableSchema` and an `IngestDef` — the declaration is data, not code.

## §4 · Two findings

**1 · Conservation cannot see any of this, and that is the whole reason §3.3 exists [D].** The field's conservation law compares the ledger to the lattice: every unit that was *ingested* lands in exactly one cell. Under `--reducer last` it holds perfectly — nothing is lost, nothing is double-counted, the projection is exact. The number was simply wrong before it reached the ledger. A store whose only ingest check is conservation is checking that it did not lose what it was given, not that it was given the truth. QC-2 F8 said this; running it makes it concrete.

**2 · The defect hides on 90 percent of the wire [M/D].** Olist has 112,650 items across 98,666 orders — 1.14 items per order — so about 90 percent of orders have a single line item, and on a single-row group `sum` and `last` **agree exactly**. The lie arm disagrees on 1,970 of 20,000 sampled cells: 9.85 percent, matching the multi-item share. A wrong reducer therefore looks right on nine rows out of ten, which is precisely why it survives review and why the reducer has no default. Both halves are oracles: `red.lie.last_is_not_sum_on_a_multi_row_group` and `red.lie.but_they_agree_on_one_row_which_is_how_it_hides`.

## §5 · Deltas against the blueprint

1. **Money is parsed as an integer at the boundary.** `"58.90"` becomes 5890 minor units by reading digits, never by `atof` and a multiply. §3.3 does not say how a decimal column crosses into the store, and a float there puts rounding error into the one column a cap fold sums.
2. **`first` and `last` are declared dependencies on source order.** They are in the closed set because §3.3 puts them there, and a class that uses one is declaring that the source's row order is meaningful. Naming that out loud is the reason to keep them rather than to remove them.
3. **The ingest declaration has its own pin**, mixed length-prefixed like the class pin, so a reducer changing from `last` to `sum` invalidates everything derived under the old one. §3.3 pins "every field of the class including the predicate operators, cls, flags, horizons and reversibility" and does not mention the ingest declaration; without it, the single most consequential field in this receipt would move without moving a pin.

## §6 · A limitation this rung exposed

**A bulk ingest pays one flush per row.** `Transactor::write` commits its own batch, so 20,000 orders is 20,000 `FlushFileBuffers` calls at the ~2 ms toll R0.1 measured — the group commit R0.1 built is unreachable from the write path. §4.8 says "batch writes are pipelined requests with client request ids, not a second verb", so the intended answer is pipelining at the socket; R0.3's server serves one request at a time and cannot pipeline. Neither is wrong, and together they mean bulk load is currently slower than the storage layer allows by more than an order of magnitude. Named here rather than left to be discovered as a mystery.
